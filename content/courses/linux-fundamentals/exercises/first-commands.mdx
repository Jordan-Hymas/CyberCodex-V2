## Learn

Linux always knows which user is running each command. whoami prints your username, and id adds your numeric user ID (UID) and your groups.

Your username is the human-friendly name. The UID is the number Linux actually uses to decide what you may read or change. Groups give extra access to shared files. This lab has one practice user, user, so nothing you do here touches your real computer or anyone else's environment.

## Why it matters

On any system you break into or defend, the first question is "who am I running as?". Your user decides what you can access.

## Example

```sh
whoami      # prints your username
id          # prints your UID, GID and groups
```

Example filenames illustrate syntax; inspect your mission files before running them.

## Your mission

Dispatches are sorted by username. Find out who you are, then read the dispatch addressed to you.

1. Run whoami to find your username.
2. Run ls dispatches to see who has mail.
3. Read the file named after your username: cat dispatches/&lt;your username&gt;.txt

## Common mistake

The user shown in the lab isn't your CyberCodex account or email. It's the practice user inside your environment.
