## Learn

### Just the start, just the end

Often you don't need a whole file. `head` prints the first lines and `tail` prints the last lines. Both show 10 lines unless you say otherwise.

```sh
head events.log   # the first 10 lines
```

### Choosing how many: -n

`-n NUMBER` sets how many lines you get. `tail -n 1` gives only the very last line, `head -n 3` the first three.

```sh
head -n 3 events.log
tail -n 2 events.log
tail -n 1 events.log
```

### Why tail matters for logs

Programs add new log entries at the bottom, so the newest events are always at the end of a log. `tail` is how you see what just happened. The top of a file, on the other hand, often holds headers, column names or sample lines, which is what `head` is good for: a quick look at a file's format before you process it.

On a full Linux system, `tail -f` keeps the file open and prints new lines as they're written, which is how admins watch a log live.

## Command cheat sheet

- `head FILE`: First 10 lines.
- `head -n N FILE`: First N lines.
- `tail FILE`: Last 10 lines.
- `tail -n N FILE`: Last N lines. `tail -n 1` is the newest log entry.

## Your mission

dispatches.txt is a running log. The newest dispatch is always added at the bottom. Don't trust the header.

1. Peek at the top with head to see what kind of file it is.
2. Print only the newest (last) line.

## Common mistake

tail -n 1 selects one line, not one character.
